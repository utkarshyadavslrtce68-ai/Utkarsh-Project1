Natural language processing (NLP) is an interdisciplinary subfield of computer science and information retrieval. It is primarily concerned with giving computers the ability to support and manipulate speech and text in ways that are similar to human communication. NLP combines computational linguistics—rule-based modeling of human language—with statistical, machine learning, and deep learning models. These technologies enable computers to process human language in the form of text or voice data and to understand its full meaning, complete with the speaker or writer's intent and sentiment.

Spelling and grammar checking is one of the foundational applications in computational linguistics. Historically, early checkers used dictionary lookups and rule-based heuristic pattern matching. Minimum edit distance algorithms, such as the Levenshtein distance and Damerau-Levenshtein distance, calculate the smallest number of insertion, deletion, substitution, and transposition operations required to transform one string into another. Dynamic programming provides an optimal polynomial-time solution for this sequence alignment problem.

In 2007, Peter Norvig introduced an elegant probabilistic spelling corrector based on Bayes' theorem. Given an observed word w, the objective is to find the candidate word c that maximizes the posterior probability P(c|w). By Bayes' rule, this is proportional to P(w|c) multiplied by P(c), where P(c) represents the prior probability of the candidate word occurring in the language, and P(w|c) is the error likelihood model reflecting the probability that the writer intended word c but typed word w. Candidate generation typically examines words within an edit distance of one or two operations from the query word.

Beyond isolated non-word spelling errors, real-world text contains context-sensitive spelling mistakes where the typed token is a legitimate dictionary entry but grammatically or semantically invalid in context. Common confusion sets include homophones and homoglyphs such as their, there, and they're; its and it's; your and you're; whose and who's; affect and effect; accept and except; loose and lose; principal and principle; and complement and compliment. Resolving these real-word errors requires language modeling, utilizing n-gram sequence probabilities, part-of-speech tagging, and syntactic parsing.

Grammar checking involves detecting syntactic violations, including subject-verb agreement, article-noun number consistency, run-on sentences, sentence fragments, dangling modifiers, double negatives, split infinitives, and punctuation irregularities. Modern automated grammar error correction (GEC) architectures employ sequence-to-sequence neural networks, pre-trained bidirectional transformer encoders, and autoregressive language models to suggest natural, context-aware revisions while preserving stylistic integrity and communicative clarity.
